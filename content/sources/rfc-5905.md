## Summary

RFC 5905 (2010) defines NTPv4, the protocol most machines use to keep
their clocks in step with a time server. It covers how a client
estimates its offset from a server with four timestamps, how error
bounds grow between measurements, and when the client slews its clock
gently versus steps it to a new value.

## Key claims

- NTP carries time from primary servers, tied to a reference, down a tree of secondary servers to clients. "The purpose of the NTP protocol is to convey timekeeping information from these primary servers to secondary time servers and clients via both private networks and the public Internet." (1)
- Primary servers are within tens of microseconds. "Typical primary servers using modern machines are precise within a few tens of microseconds." (1)
- LAN clients are within a few hundred microseconds. "Typical secondary servers and clients on fast LANs are within a few hundred microseconds with poll intervals up to 1024 seconds, which was the maximum with NTPv3." (1)
- With long poll intervals, tens of milliseconds. "With NTPv4, servers and clients are precise within a few tens of milliseconds with poll intervals up to 36 hours." (1)
- NTP runs over UDP on purpose; retries would add delay error. "Reliable message delivery such as TCP [RFC0793] can actually make the delivered NTP packet less reliable since retries would increase the delay value and other errors." (1)
- The offset is computed from four timestamps: client send (T1), server receive (T2), server send (T3), client receive (T4). "theta = T(B) - T(A) = 1/2 * [(T2-T1) + (T3-T4)]" (8)
- The round-trip delay leaves out the server's processing time. "delta = T(ABA) = (T4-T1) - (T3-T2)." (8)
- Dispersion is the maximum error of a measurement. "The dispersion (epsilon) represents the maximum error inherent in the measurement." (4)
- Between measurements the error bound grows at a fixed rate. "With the default value PHI = 15 ppm, this amounts to about 1.3 s per day." (9.2)
- Offsets over 125 ms are stepped (the clock is set directly), smaller ones adjust gradually. "STEP means the offset is less than the panic threshold, but greater than the step threshold STEPT (125 ms)." (11.2.3)
- Smaller offsets are a normal update. "ADJ means the offset is less than the step threshold and thus a valid update." (11.2.3)
- A step only happens after the large offset has lasted 900 s. "A step clock action is implemented by setting the clock directly, but this is done only after the stepout threshold WATCH (900 s) when the offset is more than the step threshold STEPT (.125 s)." (11.3)
- Very large offsets make the daemon give up. "PANIC means the offset is greater than the panic threshold PANICT (1000 s)" (11.2.3)
- Primary servers are kept right by wire or radio links to national standards. "The NTP subnet model includes a number of widely accessible primary time servers synchronized by wire or radio to national standards." (1)
- Time flows down a tree. "Servers and clients are configured such that values flow towards clients from the primary servers at the root via branching secondary servers." (1)
- NTP packets are UDP datagrams. "The NTP packet is a UDP datagram [RFC0768]." (7.3)
- Past the panic threshold the daemon should exit. "SHOULD cause the program to exit with a diagnostic message to the system log." (11.2.3)
- The reply carries the client's send time and the server's receive and send times. "Origin Timestamp (org): Time at the client when the request departed for the server" (7.3; also Receive Timestamp and Transmit Timestamp, the server's arrival and departure times)
- The four-timestamp exchange is drawn as Figure 15. "The on-wire protocol uses four timestamps numbered t1 through t4 and three state variables org, rec, and xmt, as shown in Figure 15." (8)
- The maximum error includes half the round-trip delay, which is how much a lopsided path could shift the offset. "The synchronization distance (LAMBDA) equal to EPSILON + DELTA / 2 represents the maximum error due to all causes." (4)

## My notes

- The offset formula averages the two one-way trips, so it is exact only
  when both directions take the same time. The RFC doesn't spell that
  out in section 8; it follows from the formula.
- The numbers in section 1 are "typical", not guarantees.
