## Summary

A two-page treatise (copyright 1998 to 2000) of 18 short points about
failure in complex systems, written from patient safety and widely read
in operations. The points used here are about why a single root cause
doesn't exist and why hindsight distorts judgments of what people did.

## Key claims

- Catastrophe needs several failures together. "Each of these small failures is necessary to cause catastrophe but only the combination is sufficient to permit failure." (point 3)
- Complex systems always carry latent failures. "The complexity of these systems makes it impossible for them to run without multiple flaws being present." (point 4)
- Root-cause attribution is wrong. "Post-accident attribution to a ‘root cause’ is fundamentally wrong." (point 7, heading)
- Root-cause stories come from a need to blame. "The evaluations based on such reasoning as ‘root cause’ do not reflect a technical understanding of the nature of failure but rather the social, cultural need to blame specific, localized forces or events for outcomes." (point 7)
- Hindsight bias. "Knowledge of the outcome makes it seem that events leading to the outcome should have appeared more salient to practitioners at the time than was actually the case." (point 8)
- Every action is a gamble made under uncertainty; successes are gambles too. "But all practitioner actions are actually gambles, that is, acts that take place in the face of uncertain outcomes." (point 10)
- Post-accident fixes often add coupling and complexity. "Instead of increasing safety, post-accident remedies usually increase the coupling and complexity of the system." (point 15)
- (For postmortems.) The treatise is a list of 18 numbered points, from "Complex systems are intrinsically hazardous systems." (point 1) to "Failure free operations require experience with failure." (point 18)
