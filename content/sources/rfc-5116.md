## Summary

Defines authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) as an
interface: encrypt takes a key, a nonce, plaintext and associated data;
decrypt returns the plaintext or a failure. Sets the rules for nonces
and defines AES-GCM and AES-CCM as the first AEAD algorithms (2008).

## Key claims

- Authenticated encryption gives confidentiality plus a way to check integrity and authenticity. "Authenticated encryption [BN00] is a form of encryption that, in addition to providing confidentiality for the plaintext that is encrypted, provides a way to check its integrity and authenticity." (1)
- AEAD also authenticates data that isn't encrypted. "adds the ability to check the integrity and authenticity of some Associated Data (AD), also called \"additional authenticated data\", that is not encrypted." (1)
- One algorithm replaces the cipher-plus-MAC pair. "In this concept, the cipher and MAC are replaced by an Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD) algorithm." (1.1)
- The key must be random. "A secret key K, which MUST be generated in a way that is uniformly random or pseudorandom." (2.1)
- Nonces must never repeat under one key. "Each nonce provided to distinct invocations of the Authenticated Encryption operation MUST be distinct, for any particular value of the key" (2.1)
- Twelve-byte nonces are the norm. "Otherwise, the number of octets in the nonce SHOULD be twelve (12)." (2.1)
- Decryption either returns the plaintext or FAIL. "It has only a single output, either a plaintext value P or a special symbol FAIL that indicates that the inputs are not authentic." (2.2)
- Several devices sharing a key must coordinate nonces. "When there are multiple devices performing encryption using a single key, those devices must coordinate to ensure that the nonces are unique." (3.1)
- A long-lived key needs its nonce counter saved to non-volatile storage. "If a secret key will be used for a long period of time, e.g., across multiple reboots, then the nonce will need to be stored in non-volatile memory." (3.1)
- Changing keys avoids many nonce problems. "Many problems with nonce reuse can be avoided by changing a key in a situation in which nonce coordination is difficult." (3.1)
- Recommended nonce format: a fixed field per encrypter plus a counter that goes up by one. "The nonce is formatted as illustrated in Figure 1, with the initial octets consisting of a Fixed field, and the final octets consisting of a Counter field." and "The Counter part SHOULD be equal to zero for the first nonce, and increment by one for each successive nonce that is generated." (3.2)
- Each device sharing a key needs its own fixed field. "If different devices are performing encryption with a single key, then each distinct device MUST use a distinct Fixed field, to ensure the uniqueness of the nonces." (3.2)
- AES-GCM comes with 128- and 256-bit keys. "Mode [GCM] with 128- and 256-bit keys" (1.2)
- AES-128-GCM adds a 16-byte tag. "An AEAD_AES_128_GCM ciphertext is exactly 16 octets longer than its corresponding plaintext." (5.1)
- AES-256-GCM is the same algorithm with a 32-byte key, so the 16-byte expansion holds for it too. "This algorithm is identical to AEAD_AES_128_GCM, but with the following differences: K_LEN is 32 octets, instead of 16 octets" (5.2)
- Nonce reuse in GCM breaks confidentiality and all integrity under that key. "undermines the confidentiality of the plaintexts protected in those two invocations, and undermines all of the authenticity and integrity protection provided by that key." (5.1.1)
- An attacker gets the XOR of the plaintexts and can then forge messages. "First, a loss of confidentiality ensues because he will be able to reconstruct the bitwise exclusive-or of the two plaintext values." and "Knowledge of this key makes subsequent forgeries trivial." (5.1.1)
