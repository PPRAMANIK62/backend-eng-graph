## Summary

The IETF's Best Current Practice (BCP 197) on queue management in
routers and other network devices. It explains why plain tail-drop
queues sit full, what active queue management (AQM) buys you, and
recommends that deployments use AQM. It replaces RFC 2309 (1998) and
drops that RFC's advice to run RED by default.

## Key claims

- Status: Best Current Practice, obsoletes RFC 2309. (header)
- Queuing happens when arrivals beat departures; some is normal, too much adds delay. "Queueing occurs whenever the arrival rate of data at the ingress to a device exceeds the current egress rate." (1.2)
- Tail drop: accept packets until the queue is full, then drop new arrivals. "This technique is known as "tail drop", since the packet that arrived most recently (i.e., the one on the tail of the queue) is dropped when the queue is full." (2)
- Tail drop keeps queues full, because it only signals once the queue is full. "The "tail drop" discipline allows queues to maintain a full (or, almost full) status for long periods of time, since tail drop signals congestion (via a packet drop) only when the queue has become full." (2, Full Queues)
- A burst hitting a nearly full queue loses several packets from the same flow. "If the queue is full or almost full, an arriving burst will cause multiple packets to be dropped from the same flow." (2, Full Queues)
- Buffers are for bursts; small normal queues give both lower delay and higher throughput. "The counterintuitive result is that maintaining queues that are normally small can result in higher throughput as well as lower end-to-end delay." (2, Full Queues)
- Queue limits should be sized for bursts, not for steady state. "queue limits should not reflect the steady-state queues we want to be maintained in the network; instead, they should reflect the size of bursts that a network device needs to absorb." (2)
- Large bursts disturb the TCP ACK clock of other flows. "A large burst of packets can delay other packets, disrupting the control loop (e.g., the pacing of flows by the TCP ACK clock)" (2, Mitigating the Impact of Packet Bursts)
- The AQM fix: drop or ECN-mark before the queue is full. "The solution to the full-queues problem is for network devices to drop or ECN-mark packets before a queue becomes full, so that hosts can respond to congestion before buffers overflow." (2)
- AQM can control queue length or time spent in the queue. "Active Queue Management (AQM) is a method that allows network devices to control the queue length or the mean time that a packet spends in a queue." (2)
- RED worked when tuned, but tuning was hard, so it was rarely turned on. "RED has not been enabled by default, and its present use in the Internet is limited." (1.2)
- Queue management (drop/mark) and scheduling (which packet next) are different jobs that can be combined. "queue management algorithms manage the length of packet queues by marking or dropping packets when necessary or appropriate, while scheduling algorithms determine which packet to send next" (1.2)
- Endpoint congestion control alone isn't enough. "there is a limit to how much control can be accomplished from the edges of the network." (1.1)
- Recommendation 4.1: operational deployments SHOULD use AQM. (4.1 heading)
