## Summary

NIST's definition of attribute-based access control (2014, with
editorial updates in 2019). It places ABAC after identity-based access
(ACLs) and RBAC, defines subject, object and environment attributes,
and names the policy decision point (PDP) and enforcement point (PEP).
It's frank about what ABAC costs: many more trust relationships.

## Key claims

- ACLs (identity-based access): each object has its own list, decided before any request. "Each object needs its own ACL and set of privileges assigned to each subject." (§2, IBAC/ACLs)
- ACLs drift. "Failure to remove or revoke access over time leads to users accumulating privileges." (§2, IBAC/ACLs)
- RBAC: pre-defined roles carry privileges; subjects are assigned to roles. "Role-Based Access Control model (RBAC) [FK92, ANSI359, SCFY96] employs pre-defined roles that carry a specific set of privileges associated with them and to which subjects are assigned." (§2, RBAC)
- A role can be seen as just one attribute. "Note that a role may be viewed as a subject attribute that is evaluated by the access control mechanism and around which object access policy is generated." (§2, RBAC)
- ACLs and RBAC are special cases of ABAC. "ACLs work on the attribute of “identity”. RBAC works on the attribute of “role”." (§2, ABAC)
- What makes ABAC different: boolean policies over many attributes. "The key difference with ABAC is the concept of policies that express a complex Boolean rule set that can evaluate many different attributes." (§2, ABAC)
- ABAC decides from subject attributes, object attributes, environment conditions and policy, at request time. "the ABAC engine can make an access control decision based on the assigned attributes of the requester, the assigned attributes of the object, environment conditions, and a set of policies that are specified in terms of those attributes and conditions." (§2, ABAC)
- Environment conditions: time, location, threat level. "Examples of environment conditions include time, location, threat level, and temperature." (§1.4 Notes on Terminology)
- PDP decides, PEP enforces. "the access control mechanism often employs a policy decision point (PDP) to render a decision, a policy enforcement point (PEP) to enforce the decision" (§1.4)
- Role explosion. "Trying to implement these kinds of access control decisions would require the creation of numerous roles that are ad hoc and limited in membership, leading to what is often termed “role explosion”." (§2.1)
- RBAC doesn't handle multi-factor decisions well, e.g. location or recent HIPAA training. "RBAC does not easily support multi-factor decisions" (§2.1)
- With ACL or RBAC, a changed requirement is hard to trace to every place it's implemented. "if the AC requirement is changed, it may be difficult to identify all the places where the ACL or RBAC implementation needs to be updated." (§2, ABAC)
- ABAC needs more trust. "there are many more complex trust relationships required for ABAC to work properly." (§3.1.2)
- With ABAC, you depend on whoever issues the attributes. "By assuming that attributes are issued appropriately, the ABAC system is partially dependent upon the attribute-issuing authorities." (§3.1.2)
- XACML is one ABAC framework; its reference architecture has PDPs, PEPs, PAPs and PIPs. (§2, ABAC)
- With ACLs the owner or admin is the root of trust; with ABAC trust comes from many parties. "ACLs are established by the object owner or administrator, who ultimately enforces the object access rules by provisioning access to the object through addition of a user to an ACL. In ABAC, the root of trust is derived from many sources, such as Subject Attribute Authorities or Policy Developers." (§3.1.2)
- Subject attributes include clearance. "nationality, and security clearance" (§2.3 Basic ABAC Concepts)
